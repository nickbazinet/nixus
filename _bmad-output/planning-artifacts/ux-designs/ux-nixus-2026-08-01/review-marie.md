# Marie's Review — Nixus UX Spec (EXPERIENCE.md + DESIGN.md, 2026-08-01)

**Reviewer persona:** Marie, 42. Eleven years in a spreadsheet. Mortgage, TFSA, RRSP, chequing, savings, some crypto her nephew set up. Runs a small business. Fifteen minutes, no patience for feeling dumb, will not file a bug — will close it and say nothing, or say "it seemed nice" if asked directly.

**Builder's question:** *What almost made you close it in the first 10 minutes?*

**Overall verdict:** This spec is the best-argued, most self-aware UX document I've seen for this product — it diagnoses its own predecessor's failures with real precision (60–80 click onboarding, three independent month states, JetBrains Mono on a mortgage payment). But it is written by and for someone who already knows what "trailing average," "fixed commitment," and "TFSA" mean. Marie is named as the primary user on line 8 of EXPERIENCE.md and then the document routinely reasons in the vocabulary of someone who *isn't* Marie. The single biggest risk is not a UI bug — it's that the spec's own honesty mechanisms (badges, caveats, disclaimers) read to Marie as **the app hedging about whether it works**, at the exact moment (first 10 minutes) she has zero trust reserve built up to spend on decoding hedges.

## 1. Onboarding walkthrough, step by step, as Marie

Source: EXPERIENCE.md § Onboarding & Seeding (lines 182–202) and Flow 1 (lines 250–262).

**Step 1 — Welcome.**
> "What Nixus is, what it isn't (no bank sync, desktop only, AI needs your own key), and the required tray explanation: 'Nixus stays in your menu bar so reminders keep working.'"

Marie's read: *"OK... no bank sync — good, I wasn't going to give it my banking password anyway. Desktop only — fine, I'm on my laptop. 'AI needs your own key' — what key? A key to what? I don't have a key. Is this going to ask me to sign up for something technical before I've even seen the app?"*

This is the first hesitation point. "AI needs your own key" is Dev-speak dropped into a screen meant to reassure a nervous first-timer. It's honest, which the spec values, but it's honest in the wrong dialect — it introduces a concept (API credentials) she doesn't have and doesn't know she doesn't have, with no immediate resolution ("but you don't need this to get started" is implied by the later fork, not stated here).

Then: *"Nixus stays in your menu bar."* Marie's spreadsheet doesn't live anywhere. It's a file. The idea that this app will now be **running in the background on her computer permanently** is new information delivered as a footnote, in the same breath as "your data stays on your machine." A slightly suspicious reader (and Marie, at minute one, with a friend's unsolicited link, is exactly that) parses this as "so it's always watching," even though the intent is the opposite (local, no sync). **This line needs to do more reassurance work, not less** — it currently reads like a permissions request with no "why," which is the classic close-the-tab trigger.

Severity: **medium.** Location: EXPERIENCE.md line 190. Fix: split into two sentences with the reassurance first: *"Nixus stays open in your menu bar — that's what lets it remind you about bills even when the window is closed. It's not sending anything anywhere; nothing leaves your computer."* And move "AI needs your own key" out of welcome entirely, or append "— skip this, you don't need it to start" inline.

**Step 2 — The fork: "Do you have a statement handy?"**

Marie has a Visa PDF. She picks Import. This is the strongest moment in the whole spec and it is correctly identified as the happy path. No hesitation here — the question is concrete and she has an immediate yes/no answer, unlike "let's set your budget" which has no natural first move.

But look at the other two options as she'd actually read them, side by side:

> - Pick a starting point *(no AI needed)* → Renter · Homeowner · Homeowner with car.
> - Start from scratch *(secondary, for Dev)*.

If Marie *doesn't* have a statement handy (very plausible — a PDF sitting in Downloads is a specific and not universal situation), she now has to choose between "Renter / Homeowner / Homeowner with car" — three boxes that don't obviously map to a woman with a mortgage, a rental duplex she may or may not own, kids, a business. She owns a home. Fine, that one's easy. But what if she's a homeowner with a car she pays cash for and no loan — does "Homeowner with car" imply a car payment category she doesn't need? The spec says templates get "editable targets," which resolves it, but nothing on this screen tells her that up front, so the hesitation is real even if brief: *"None of these are quite me, but I guess Homeowner is closest."* Low friction, but a friction, and it's friction at the fork — the highest-leverage moment in the whole flow.

Severity: **low-medium.** Location: EXPERIENCE.md line 193. Fix: one line under the template names — *"You can add, remove, or rename anything after — this just saves you typing."*

**Step 3 — Import stepper.**
> "Reading your statement → Finding transactions → Sorting into categories → Done." Twenty-two seconds.

No hesitation. This is exactly right — plain verbs, visible progress, short duration. Marie's spreadsheet has never done this for her; this is the moment the app earns something. Nothing to fix.

**Step 4 — Review.**
> "47 transactions found. 41 already sorted. Three likely duplicates are excluded... Six need her."

Marie's read: *"Wait — 'likely duplicates excluded'? Excluded from what? Did it drop three of my transactions? Are they gone, or just hidden? What if it's wrong and one of those was real?"*

This is a real hesitation the spec doesn't fully resolve at the UI-copy level. The State Patterns table (line 175) says duplicates are "auto-deselected with '3 likely duplicates excluded — review'" — that's better than "excluded" alone, because "auto-deselected" at least implies reversibility (a checkbox, not a deletion), but Flow 1's narrative prose says "excluded," which is the scarier word, and that's the word a reader will remember. **Word choice inconsistency between the flow narrative and the state pattern is itself a risk** — whichever screen actually ships this copy needs the gentler, reversible-sounding version, and it needs to be legible in three words, not require her to open a review panel to discover nothing was deleted.

Severity: **high.** Location: EXPERIENCE.md line 257 vs line 175. Fix: standardize on "3 likely duplicates found — unchecked, review if you want them" everywhere, and make sure the checkbox affordance is visually obvious next to the word "excluded"/"deselected" so she doesn't have to trust the adjective.

Continuing: "one line the AI couldn't read, which asks her to fill it in rather than presenting a blank form." Good — this is the Voice and Tone table's "We couldn't read this line — add it yourself?" (line 87), which is warm and low-stakes. No hesitation here. This is the spec at its best: admitting failure without making her feel like she caused it.

**Step 5 — Commit.**
> "She clicks Add 44 transactions. A quiet line reads: We'll remember these for next month."

No hesitation. Good microcopy — it tells her *why* the categorization work she just did matters, which is the single best trust-builder in the whole onboarding, because it reframes "I have to correct this again next month" fear before she has it.

**Step 6 — The budget "climax."**
> "'Here's a budget based on what you actually spent. Adjust anything?' — twelve categories, already filled in... She raises Groceries by fifty dollars, leaves everything else, and clicks Looks good."

This is genuinely the strongest single moment described in the document — a budget with her real numbers already in it, zero typing. If this ships as described, it is a legitimate "oh, that's actually useful" moment for someone who has spent eleven years building this exact artifact by hand. No hesitation. This is worth protecting above almost anything else in the spec — see finding §7.

**Step 7 — Today loads.**
> "$1,038.77 left for the next 12 days. Restaurants is over by $86. Her next action is to build an emergency fund, with a note that it's based on her completed months."

Here is where a first-time reader hesitates, and it's subtle: she has just spent five minutes importing one credit card statement. She has **not** told the app her income, her savings balances, her TFSA, her RRSP, her mortgage principal. Yet the very first thing Today tells her, unprompted, on minute six, is "build an emergency fund" — with a specific figure ("3.2 months of expenses covered," per the mock in directions-3.html) that the app cannot possibly know yet, because she hasn't entered any account balances. Either this number is quietly wrong/placeholder-ish for a fresh import (in which case showing it at all this early is dishonest exactly where the spec claims to be maximally honest), or the flow silently assumes accounts got added somewhere the narrative skips. Marie's actual reaction, if the number is stale or estimated: *"How does it know that? I haven't told it anything about my savings."* That's the first crack in trust the spec doesn't account for.

Severity: **high.** Location: EXPERIENCE.md line 261, cross-reference "Not enough history" state pattern at line 170 and directions-3.html's "Not enough history yet" empty state. Fix: Flow 1 step 7 should explicitly land on the **"Not enough history yet"** state for a same-day import (she has one statement, not three months), not on a populated emergency-fund figure. The flow as written contradicts the spec's own `data_sufficient: false` rule two sections earlier. This is an internal consistency bug in the document, not just a tone issue — fix the flow narrative to match the state machine.

**Where she'd actually reach for the close button in this walkthrough:** not any single step, but the seam between step 1's "AI needs your own key" (a term she doesn't have a referent for) and step 7's confident emergency-fund figure (a number she has no way to verify and didn't feel she provided the inputs for). Neither alone is fatal. Together, in a 6-minute span, they produce a "does this thing actually know what it's talking about, or is it just performing confidence?" doubt — which is a first-degree threat to a self-consciously-behind-on-money user's trust.

## 3. The four destinations — can Marie find the answer?

| Her question | Where she'd click | Does it work? |
|---|---|---|
| "How much did I spend on groceries last month?" | Spending → Transactions, filter by category + date, or Spending → Budget (Groceries row shows spent-to-date) | **Yes, reachable**, though she has to guess whether "Budget" or "Transactions" has it. Budget shows the current month's total per category by design; "last month" requires the period selector to actually work globally, which the spec flags as *currently broken* in the shipped app (three independent `useState` hooks, line 67) and *fixed* in this spine ("Period is global"). If it doesn't, she clicks Budget, sees June, can't get to May, and this is exactly the "review last month" failure the spec names as structurally broken today. **This is a load-bearing fix — if it slips, this specific question fails for her on day one.** |

| "What's that charge I don't recognize?" | Spending → Transactions, search or sort by amount | Reachable, and Flow 2 demonstrates exactly this journey working well. This is the best-supported of the four questions in the current spec. |

**On "Wealth":** Marie's own words, if asked what "Wealth" means to her: *"Wealth is Warren Buffett. I have a mortgage and a car loan. I'm not wealthy, I'm keeping my head above water."* This is a real terminology risk. The destination *absorbs* Accounts, Assets, Net Worth, and Financial Health (table, line 51) — which is the right grouping conceptually (things you own vs. things you spend), but the label asks her to self-apply a word she associates with people richer than her. It doesn't have to be a dealbreaker (she'll click around and find it), but it's friction exactly where the spec claims destinations should be self-evident ("Ten Finance tabs collapse to four destinations," positioned as a clarity win). If it takes her three tries to find her credit card balance, the "clarity win" hasn't landed for the primary named user.

Severity: **medium-high.** Location: EXPERIENCE.md line 51, Information Architecture table. Fix: this is a naming decision worth user-testing before lock, not something to fix from a desk. Candidate alternatives to test: "Accounts," "My Money," "Net Worth" (promoting the most concrete sub-item to be the label). If "Wealth" survives testing with people like Marie, keep it — but the spec currently asserts the four-destination *structure* is validated (it cites `InnerTabNav.tsx`'s existing grouping as evidence) without asserting the *labels* are validated, and "Wealth" is the label most likely to alienate the named persona.

**On "Insights":** Less risky than "Wealth" because it's vaguer in a way that's actually forgiving — "Insights" doesn't promise anything specific, so it can't be *wrong*, but it also doesn't tell her what she'll get. Given the spec itself flags Insights sub-surfaces as "FR-mandated with zero recorded design" (Open Items, line 300), this is a known gap, not a new finding — but worth stating for Marie's sake: if she ever clicks Insights expecting an answer to "what's changing," and finds Spending Trends / Year Summary / Projection with no plain-language framing (because none has been designed yet), that's a second destination-naming risk stacked on the first, in a section the spec admits isn't designed yet.

## 4. The three-question dashboard — helpful or judging?

> "Can I spend? / What needs attention? / What should I do?"

Read cold, in Marie's voice: *"'Can I spend?' — OK, that's just... my number. Fine. 'What needs attention?' — that sounds like a parent asking what's wrong. 'What should I do?' — I didn't come here for advice, I came here to see my numbers."*

This is the crux of finding this document should take most seriously. The three questions are a strong **organizing principle** for the builder (they compress 29 numbers into three cards, which is a real usability win, and the spec is right that this beats a wall of stats). But the *literal phrasing*, read by someone who is "slightly ashamed of her finances" (per the brief), risks landing as three separate parental check-ins rather than three neutral facts. The tone risk is concentrated in card two and three, not card one:

- **"What needs attention?"** — attention from whom? The app is positioning itself as the one paying attention, which (intentionally or not) implies she wasn't. For someone already a little embarrassed, this is closer to "what did you mess up" than the designers likely intended. The badges underneath it (Over / Due soon / Stale) do the actual work fine — they're neutral, factual. It's the *header question* that carries unwanted judgment, not the content.
- **"What should I do?" + Next-action card** ("Build your emergency fund") — this is well-softened by good writing elsewhere (no exclamation marks, banned per line 94; "earn delight, don't manufacture it") — but a card that tells her, unprompted, on day one, what her top financial priority ought to be, before she's asked for advice, risks reading as presumptuous. It's *useful* (that's real and the spec is right that a deterministic, backend-owned single recommendation beats a wall of generic tips) — but usefulness and the feeling of being lectured aren't mutually exclusive, and this persona is specifically primed to receive it as the latter.

**What actually defuses this in the spec, and should be preserved/reinforced:**
- The "not-advice" disclaimer (line 137) is *always visible*, which is the correct mechanism — it's just currently scoped to "every financial-health surface," and the next-action card on Today may need the same permanent disclaimer, since that's exactly where the "should" language lives.
- "Fixed commitments... render `paid`, not warning" (line 110) is a genuinely good anti-shame design decision — a mortgage at 100% of target isn't flagged as a problem. That's the spec actively *removing* a shame-trigger the shipped app has today. Good, keep.
- Never manufacturing enthusiasm (line 94) removes the opposite failure mode (fake cheerfulness that reads as condescending when things aren't going well) — also correct and should be preserved.

Severity: **medium** (tone, not blocking, but cumulative with §1's trust gap). Location: EXPERIENCE.md line 184 (implied), directions-3.html `.q` labels. Fix: reword the second question's *label*, not its content: "What needs attention?" → "What to check" or simply "Attention" as a card title with the badges doing the talking (they already don't need the question-framing to be legible — Over/Due soon/Paid read as facts on their own). Keep "Can I spend?" as-is (it's the strongest of the three, genuinely useful, non-judgmental). For "What should I do?", consider "Suggested next step" as the header — same information, less imperative mood, and it doubles as a soft signal that this is a suggestion, not a to-do the app is assigning her.

## 5. Data Honesty Contract — trust-builder or wall of caveats?

For a *second-session* user, or a beta tester specifically recruited to give friction reports, the Data Honesty Contract (line 123-137) is unambiguously good — it's the kind of document that makes an engineer or an informed early adopter trust the product *more*, because it names its own limitations before a user discovers them the hard way. That's real, and it should absolutely stay as an internal design contract.

For **Marie specifically, in her first 15 minutes**, the picture is more mixed, because she never reads this table — she only experiences its *symptoms*: a dash in the Account column, a "Stale" badge, a search box that only finds merchants, a permanent not-advice disclaimer she didn't ask for. The question is whether those symptoms, encountered cold and back-to-back, read as "this app is honest" or "this app is unfinished."

Flow 2 (line 264-275) is the section that actually tests this, and its own conclusion is telling: *"two more rows tagged `from import` with a dash in the Account column. Instead of reading it as missing data, the badge tells her why... She was not confused, and she did not email you about it."* This is the spec's own best-case projection, not an observed result, and it's plausible **only if** the badge copy does the explanatory work the current mock's bare "from import" text doesn't quite do (see §2's finding on that badge). If the badge under-explains even slightly, "from import" next to a dash reads exactly like "this row is broken" to someone who has never seen this design pattern before — the honesty mechanism only builds trust if it's legible *without* having read the design rationale, which by definition Marie hasn't.

The permanent, non-dismissible "not advice" disclaimer (line 137) is the riskiest single element of the whole contract for a first-session read, for a specific reason: it is legally/product-motivated, but to a user it reads as *the app hedging about whether its own emergency-fund number is trustworthy* — on the same screen, in the same ten minutes, where the app is also asking her to trust an AI-extracted, AI-categorized statement she didn't manually verify. Two separate "trust me, but also here's why you maybe shouldn't fully" signals compounding in one sitting is a real risk, even though each one individually is well-intentioned and honest.

Severity: **medium-high**, specifically for first-session trust (not a concern for retained users, who is who this contract is actually optimized for). Location: line 137, and the Today surface implied by directions-3.html's next-action card. Fix: the disclaimer's *placement*, not its existence, is the lever — keep it permanent on Financial Health (where it's load-bearing), but on Today's next-action card specifically, consider a lighter-weight rendering (a small "ⓘ educational, not advice" affordance rather than a full-sentence disclaimer competing for attention next to her first-ever recommendation) so it doesn't read as a retraction of the thing she just read. This is about visual weight and sequencing, not about removing honesty.

## 6. What's missing that eleven years of spreadsheet habit would expect

This is the question the spec answers least, because it's largely written from "what does the shipped app already have that's broken" rather than "what does a spreadsheet do that nothing here mentions." Going through what Marie's spreadsheet almost certainly does today, cross-referenced against the spec:

- **A running total / balance she can trust at a glance.** Her spreadsheet has had a live balance column for eleven years — it's the single most basic thing a ledger does. The spec explicitly and correctly *bans* a running-balance column (line 129) because the backend can't honestly support it. This is the right call given the data model, but it means the single most spreadsheet-native expectation is a **known, permanent gap**, and nothing in the spec proposes a substitute that gets her most of the way there for the *specific* task ("is my chequing account going to bounce a payment this week"). The "net flow" figure in transactions-compare.html is the closest analog, and even that's mislabeled-by-omission per §2. **This is the biggest unaddressed expectation gap in the whole document**, and it's worth the builder explicitly deciding "we will never have this, here's the alternative we offer instead, and here's how we make that trade-off legible to her" rather than leaving it as an absence.
- **Year-over-year / multi-year comparison.** Eleven years of spreadsheet rows means Marie is used to scrolling back to "what did I spend on groceries in 2019?" The spec's Insights destination absorbs "Year Summary," which is a start, but multi-year (not just this-year-vs-last) isn't discussed anywhere, and the spec itself flags Insights as undesigned (Open Items, line 300).
- **Exporting her own data back out.** A spreadsheet person's deepest habit is that the data is *hers*, in a format she can always get back to. Nothing in this spec — not Settings (explicitly flagged as unspecified, line 299), not Data Honesty, not anywhere — mentions CSV export or "get your data out." For someone whose entire relationship to money tracking is "I own this file," the absence of an explicit export promise is a real gap, distinct from backup/restore (which is mentioned as a Settings utility but is about disaster recovery, not about her being able to open her transactions in Excel if she wants to). This is exactly the kind of thing that, if she asks about it and gets no answer, sends her back to the spreadsheet permanently — because switching *to* Nixus without a guaranteed way *out* of Nixus is a bigger commitment than she signed up for.
- **Formulas / custom categories / her own logic.** A spreadsheet is infinitely bendable. Nixus's category and template system is more structured, which is mostly a *feature* (it's why the app can do things her spreadsheet can't, like auto-categorization) — but the spec should be explicit that she can rename, merge, split, and delete categories freely, because if she ever hits a wall where "the app's categories don't match how I think about my money," that's an instant credibility loss for a spreadsheet veteran who is used to unlimited flexibility. The onboarding section does say templates have "editable targets" (line 193) but doesn't say categories themselves are freely editable/creatable — that should be stated explicitly somewhere central, not just implied.
- **Multiple sheets/tabs for different purposes** (e.g., a "big purchases" tracker, a "taxes" tab, a "who owes me money" tab). Out of scope for v1 almost certainly, but worth naming as a known gap rather than a silent one, since it's the kind of thing a beta tester would mention in exactly the friction report the builder is soliciting.

Severity of the export gap specifically: **high** (it's the kind of thing that, if she thinks to ask and doesn't get an answer, ends the relationship on principle even if everything else works). Severity of the rest: **low-medium**, mostly "flag for future design," not first-10-minutes blockers.

## 7. The single thing most likely to make her close it

**The three-months data-insufficiency contradiction in Flow 1 (§1, step 7).**

Not the terminology, not "Wealth," not the disclaimer wall — those are real but survivable frictions. The thing that actually breaks the promise the whole document is built on is this: the spec spends its entire Onboarding section arguing, correctly, that the old wizard's sin was **pretending to know things it didn't** (asking her to build a budget from nothing, as if she owed the app 50 fields of trust up front). Then its own flagship walkthrough — the one the builder will show to the next beta tester, the one that's supposed to be the *fixed* version — has Today load on minute six with a confident, specific "3.2 months of expenses covered" emergency-fund figure, for a user who has imported exactly one statement and entered zero account balances. That is the *exact* failure mode — the app appearing to know something about her it hasn't earned — that Data Honesty Contract and the `data_sufficient: false` state pattern were written to prevent, contradicting the spec's own rules two sections apart in the same file.

If this ships as narrated, the moment Marie would close the app isn't a confusing button or an ugly color — it's the half-second where she thinks *"how does it know that,"* gets no good answer, and the whole "calm, honest, builder voice" promise the document leads with (line 75) quietly stops being true for her, right when she has the least reason yet to give it the benefit of the doubt. Everything else in the spec's first ten minutes is recoverable friction. This one is a credibility break, and credibility, once a first-time user notices it's fake, doesn't come back in the same session.

**Fix (repeated from §1, elevated here as the priority fix in the whole review):** Flow 1 step 7 must render the `data_sufficient: false` empty state ("Not enough history yet — you have one [month]") on a same-day single-import onboarding completion, not a populated 3.2-months figure. This is a one-paragraph edit to EXPERIENCE.md's Flow 1, and it should be treated as a spec defect to fix before this document is considered final, not a design nuance to revisit later.
